## Qu 4. Free Electron Model of Conduction in Metals

Write you answer starting on a new page for this question.
This question is about conduction in a metal. Data on the metal sodium can be found at the end of the question. (Sodium has one free electron per atom for conduction.)
Conduction in metals can be usefully discussed in terms of the free electron model. We can consider "free" electrons that move with complete freedom throughout a metallic solid, subject only to reflection at the inside surface of the conducting body. Classically the electron has a large random thermal motion with a superimposed systematic drift due to an electric or thermal gradient in the metal.

(a) If we consider the electron gas to have a classical gas distribution of velocities, using the kinetic interpretation of temperature, evaluate the RMS velocity of an electron at room temperature (300 K). This is equivalent to the thermal speed, $v_{\text {th }}$.
(b) If an electric field $E$ is applied to the conductor the electrons will drift in the opposite direction to $E$. This drift velocity $v_{\mathrm{d}}$ is lost due to random collisions of the electrons with the ion lattice, so that the average drift velocity is zero immediately after any scattering collision.
    (i) Sketch a schematic graph of $v_{\mathrm{d}}$ against $t$ for an electron in the field $E$.
    (ii) Calculate the average drift velocity of an electron in a sodium wire of cross sectional area $1 \mathrm{~mm}^{2}$ with a current of 1 A flowing.
    (iii) Sketch a path of such a conduction electron as it travels along the wire
    (iv) Calculate a value for the distance travelled parallel to the field, $x_{\mathrm{d}}$ in time $\tau_{m}$.
(c) The resistivity of a metal, $\rho$ is also often cited in terms of the electron mean free path, the distance $\lambda=v_{\text {th }} \tau_{m}$ that any electron moves by virtue of its thermal speed during the mean free time between collisions with the lattice ions, $\tau_{m}$. Derive an expression for the resistivity of a metal, in terms of $e, \lambda, n, k_{\mathrm{B}}, m_{e}, T$.
(d) The temperature dependence of the resistivity of a metal is obtained in this result. How does this dependence compare with the typical temperature dependence of $\rho$ for metals at room temperature?
(e) Using the conductivity $\sigma=1 / \rho$ for sodium, and the data given at the end of the question, calculate the mean free path of an electron in sodium at room temperature.

It is clear that this model of conduction gives the wrong temperature dependence, and including a distribution of electron velocities as for a gas does not correct the difficulty.
To glimpse an approach to a quantum model, we treat the electron as a standing wave with a de Broglie wavelength in a closed container (analogous to sound waves in a closed pipe). An electron is trapped in the metal conductor in a state represented by a standing wave with nodes at the faces. The states form energy levels in the solid (like energy levels in an atom), as illustrated in Figure 7. Due to a quantum mechanical effect (the Pauli Exclusion Principle) at most two electrons can occupy a single energy level: the next two electrons fill the next energy level, etc.

(f) If we take energy levels as determined by de Broglie waves with nodes at the surface of the metal, sketch the waves corresponding to the lowest three energy levels for six electrons trapped in a one dimensional potential well of width $\ell$.

(g) In a 3-D cube of metal of side $\ell$, the energy levels are filled up to an energy $E_{\mathrm{F}}$ by $N$ electrons. Show that the number of energy levels filled up by $N$ electrons is given by $E_{\mathrm{F}}=\frac{h^{2}}{8 \ell^{2} m_{e}}\left(n_{x}^{2}+n_{y}^{2}+n_{z}^{2}\right)$, where $n_{x}, n_{y}, n_{z}$ are the labels of levels below filled by electrons.

If we let $\left(n_{x}^{2}+n_{y}^{2}+n_{z}^{2}\right)=n_{\text {max }}^{2}$ then all of the levels are filled up to $n_{\text {max }}$, which corresponds to a radius in an octant of a sphere in a space of radius $n_{\text {max }}$ (the $n$ 's are only positive values), as shown in Figure 8. Thus at temperature $T=0 \mathrm{~K}$ all $N$ conduction electrons in a cube of side $\ell$ occupy $L$ levels, given by

$$
L=\frac{1}{8}\left(\frac{4 \pi n_{\max }^{3}}{3}\right)
$$

![](bpho-r2-2020-q4-fig02.png)
Figure 8

(h) Derive an expression for $E_{\mathrm{F}}$ in terms of $h, m_{\mathrm{e}}, N$ and volume $V=\ell^{3}$.
(i) Hence obtain a numerical value for $E_{\mathrm{F}}$ for sodium in units of eV.

At a temperature $T>0 \mathrm{~K}$ only the states near $E_{\mathrm{F}}$ can supply conduction electrons, electrons in the lower states being fixed in their energy levels by the electrons above. (There are no empty states for these lower electrons to scatter into). This leads to a disconnect between the temperature and the classical thermal energy of the electrons.

(j) The Fermi speed, $v_{\mathrm{F}}$ is defined by $\frac{1}{2} m v_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}=E_{\mathrm{F}}$, and the mean free path is now given by $\lambda=v_{\mathrm{F}} \tau_{m}$.
    (i) Calculate the value of $\lambda$ for these scattered conduction electrons at the the Fermi energy.


(ii) How many atomic sodium diameters does this length correspond to? (assume a simple cubic packing structure for sodium to calculate a diameter).
(k) Calculate the derivative $\left(\frac{\mathrm{d} n}{\mathrm{~d} E}\right)$ at $E_{\mathrm{F}}$. Hence calculate the energy separation of two states at $E=E_{\mathrm{F}}$. It may be concluded that we may consider the energy to be virtually continuous for these conduction electrons.

Data for sodium at room temperature:
Sodium has one free electron per atom for conduction.

$$
\text { conductivity } \quad \sigma=8.7 \times 10^{7} \mathrm{~m} \Omega^{-1}
$$

electron ion number density $n=2.9 \times 10^{28} \mathrm{~m}^{-3}$

\end{aligned}
$$
