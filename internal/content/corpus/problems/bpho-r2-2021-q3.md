## Qu 3. Orbits

This question is about the mechanics of circular and elliptical orbits. Consider a satellite of mass $m$ in orbit around a planet of mass $M$ such that it is in free fall and sufficiently far from the planet to be able to ignore atmospheric effects. The point of closest approach (perigee) is at a distance $r_{\mathrm{p}}$ from the centre of the planet and the highest point in the orbit (apogee) is at a distance $r_{\mathrm{a}}$ from the centre of the planet.

(a) As the orbit is not circular, the equation $F=m v^{2} / r$ does not apply at any point in the orbit. However, at the apogee and perigee the velocity vector is perpendicular to the gravitational force. Hence the angular momentum ( $=m r \omega$ where $\omega$ is the angular velocity) is constant and at those two points is simply equal to $m v_{\mathrm{a}} r_{\mathrm{a}}=m v_{\mathrm{b}} r_{\mathrm{b}}$.
Use this with the conservation of energy (kinetic + gravitational potential) to derive Newton's vis viva equation (eq. 1) to find the velocity at any given radius of orbit on the ellipse:
$$
v^{2}=G M\left(\frac{2}{r}-\frac{1}{a}\right)
$$
where $a=\left(r_{\mathrm{a}}+r_{\mathrm{p}}\right) / 2$, the semi-major axis of the ellipse, $r_{\mathrm{a}}=a(1+e)$ and $r_{\mathrm{p}}=a(1-e)$, where $e$ is the eccentricity. Note: the largest diameter of an ellipse has a length $2 a$ so that $a$ is the length of the semi major axis.
(b) In order to escape from orbit, the satellite undergoes a single short burn at a specific point in the orbit which gives it a new orbit with infinite semi-major axis. That burn creates a velocity change $\Delta v$ which is a figure of interest to engineers as it is essentially proportional to the amount of fuel burnt in a given manoeuvre.
    (i) Find expressions for the necessary $\Delta v$ to reach escape velocity from apogee ( $\Delta v_{\mathrm{a}}$ ) and perigee $\left(\Delta v_{\mathrm{p}}\right)$.
    (ii) Hence show that the ratio:
$$
\frac{\Delta v_{\mathrm{a}}}{\Delta v_{\mathrm{p}}}=\frac{\sqrt{2(1-e)}-(1-e)}{\sqrt{2(1+e)}-(1+e)}
$$
    (iii) Recalling that, for an ellipse, $0<e<1$, show that it requires a greater velocity change to launch a satellite into escape orbit from apogee than from perigee - this may be counter-intuitive!

In order to change from one circular orbital radius $\left(r_{1}\right)$ to another $\left(r_{2}\right)$ in the same orbital plane, a very efficient way is to use the two-burn Hohmann transfer. A brief burn is applied at one point in the orbit which makes the orbit elliptical. The first burn point is the perigee of the ellipse. On reaching the apogee, a second burn is applied to give the necessary velocity to make a circular orbit at the new height.

(c) Show that the velocity changes at perigee and apogee are given by:
$$
\Delta v_{\mathrm{p}}=\sqrt{\frac{G M}{r_{1}}}\left(\sqrt{\frac{2 r_{2}}{r_{1}+r_{2}}}-1\right)
$$
and
$$
\Delta v_{\mathrm{p}}=\sqrt{\frac{G M}{r_{2}}}\left(1-\sqrt{\frac{2 r_{1}}{r_{1}+r_{2}}}\right)
$$
(d) For a given initial orbit $r_{1}$, the total $\Delta v=\Delta v_{\mathrm{a}}+\Delta v_{\mathrm{p}}$ will vary with $r_{2}$.
By differentiation with respect to $r_{2}$, keeping $r_{1}$ fixed, show that the maximum value of $\Delta v$ occurs for the positive root of the equation
$$
x^{3}-15 x^{2}-9 x-1=0
$$
Where $x=r_{2} / r_{1}$. This root has the approximate value of 15.6.
In other words, it requires more energy to place the satellite in a higher orbit than it does to remove it to escape velocity, another counter-intuitive result! [4]
