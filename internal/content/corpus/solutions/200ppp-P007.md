## Hint

You can base your solution on the conservation of energy and the conditions for static equilibrium.

## Solution

(i) Let us denote the elastic constant (spring constant) of the rope by $k$ and its unstretched length by $\ell_{0}$. The maximum length of the rope is $\ell_{1}=h-h_{0}=23 \mathrm{~m}$, whilst in equilibrium it is $\ell_{2}=(23-8) \mathrm{m}=15 \mathrm{~m}$. Initially, and at the jumper's lowest position, the kinetic energy is zero. If we ignore the mass of the rope and assume that the jumper's centre of mass is half-way up his body, we can use conservation of energy to write

$$
m g h=\frac{1}{2} k\left(\ell_{1}-\ell_{0}\right)^{2} .
$$

In addition, in equilibrium,

$$
m g=k\left(\ell_{2}-\ell_{0}\right) .
$$

Dividing the two equations by each other we obtain a quadratic equation for $\ell_{0}$,

$$
\ell_{0}^{2}+2\left(h-\ell_{1}\right) \ell_{0}+\left(\ell_{1}^{2}-2 h \ell_{2}\right)=\ell_{0}^{2}+4 \ell_{0}-221=0,
$$

which gives $\ell_{0}=13 \mathrm{~m}$.
(ii) When the falling jumper attains his highest speed, his acceleration must be zero, and so this must occur at the same level as the final equilibrium position ( $\ell=\ell_{2}$ ).

Again applying the law of conservation of energy,

$$
\frac{1}{2} m v^{2}+\frac{1}{2} k\left(\ell_{2}-\ell_{0}\right)^{2}=m g\left(\ell_{2}+h_{0}\right),
$$

where the ratio $m / k$ is the same as that obtained from the equilibrium condition, namely,

$$
\frac{m}{k}=\frac{\ell_{2}-\ell_{0}}{g} .
$$

Substituting this into the energy equation, shows that the maximum speed of the jumper is $v=18 \mathrm{~m} \mathrm{~s}^{-1} \approx 65 \mathrm{~km} \mathrm{~h}^{-1}$. It is easy to see that his maximum acceleration occurs at the lowest point of the jump. Since the largest extension of the rope ( 10 m ) is five times that at the equilibrium position ( 2 m ), the greatest tension in the rope is 5 mg . So the highest net force exerted on the jumper is $4 m g$, and his maximum acceleration is $4 g$.
