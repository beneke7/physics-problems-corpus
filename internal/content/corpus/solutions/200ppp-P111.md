## Hint

Divide the hemisphere into equally thick hemispherical shells. Prove that these shells each produce the same gravitational field at the point in question.

## Solution

Imagine the hemisphere to be divided into many concentric hemispherical shells of identical thickness. What is the force exerted by these shells on a probe of unit mass sitting in the centre of the sphere? Since the mass of a shell is directly proportional to the square of its radius and the force exerted by a given-mass shell on the probe is inversely proportional to the square of its radius, the gravitational accelerations at the centre due to the different hemispherical shells are all equal (see Fig. S111.1).

![](../../sources/200ppp/figures/200ppp-196.jpg)
Fig. S111.1

If there are $n$ shells, then the mass of the outermost shell is $2 \pi R^{2}(R / n) \rho$, where $R$ is the radius of the asteroid and $\rho$ is its density. The total gravitational field due to the hemisphere is $n$ times that due to its outermost shell, i.e. it is the same as if the surface of the hemisphere had mass $M=2 \pi R^{3} \rho$. This mass is, in fact, three times the actual mass of the hemisphere.

What force is exerted by this hemispherical shell of mass $M$ on the probe? Considering only its magnitude, the force is the same as that exerted by the unit-mass probe on the shell, i.e. $p=G\left(M / 2 \pi R^{2}\right)\left(1 / R^{2}\right)=G \rho / R$ per unit surface area. To integrate this effect over the whole shell, the situation can be compared with that of finding the force exerted by a liquid at pressure $p$ on a similar hemispherical shell (see Fig. S111.2).

![](../../sources/200ppp/figures/200ppp-197.jpg)
Fig. S111.2

Since the resultant force of a liquid acting on a complete hemisphere would be zero, the total force acting on its curved surface would be the same in magnitude as the force acting on its plane surface. Thus $g=p \pi R^{2}=G \rho R \pi$ (see Fig. S111.3).

![](../../sources/200ppp/figures/200ppp-198.jpg)
Fig. S111.3

Now consider the initial gravitational acceleration,

$$
g_{0}=G \frac{4 \pi R^{3}}{3} \rho \frac{1}{R^{2}},
$$

at the surface of the asteroid. The value calculated above is therefore $g= \frac{3}{4} g_{0}=7.36 \mathrm{~cm} \mathrm{~s}^{-2}$. Knowing the density of titanium, the (original) radius of the asteroid can be determined as $R \approx 78 \mathrm{~km}$.
