## Hint

Find the maximum amount of hydrogen that the container could have contained initially without bursting. The material from which the container is made may be chosen freely, but only from real materials.

## Solution

Hydrogen of pressure $p$ is enclosed in a spherical container of radius $R$ with walls of thickness $d$. Let $\rho$ be the density of the wall material and $\sigma$ its tensile strength, as illustrated in the figure.
![](../../sources/200ppp/figures/200ppp-202.jpg)

We first calculate the maximum pressure the container can sustain without bursting. If the container were cut in two, the gas would push the two pieces apart with a force of $p \pi R^{2}$ (the external pressure is negligible compared with $p$ ). This force must not exceed the product of the surface area of the cut, $2 \pi R d$, and the tensile strength, i.e.

$$
p \pi R^{2}<2 \pi R \sigma d
$$

If the gas is released into a large balloon, its pressure decreases to $p_{0}$, the external atmospheric pressure. Its final volume is therefore

$$
V=\frac{4 \pi R^{3}}{3} \frac{p}{p_{0}} .
$$

This volume of gas certainly cannot lift a load any greater than $V \rho_{\text {air }} g$ (where $\rho_{\text {air }}$ is the density of air), as the weight of the balloon and the hydrogen have still to be subtracted from the upthrust.

The weight of the container is $4 \pi R^{2} d \rho g$. In accordance with the above reasoning, this cannot be greater than the force available to lift it, i.e.

$$
4 \pi R^{2} d \rho g<\frac{4 \pi R^{3}}{3} \frac{p}{p_{0}} \rho_{\mathrm{air}} g .
$$

Inequalities (1) and (2) give a relationship between the properties of the
container material and the pressure and density of the external air,

$$
\frac{\sigma}{\rho}>\frac{3 p_{0}}{2 \rho_{\text {air }}}
$$

It is interesting that neither the radius, nor the thickness of the walls of the container occur in (3)! (A thicker-walled container can sustain a higher pressure but has a greater weight.) Despite leafing through several tables of physical properties, we could find no material that would fulfil condition (3). None of the materials known today are strong enough, relative to their densities, to be lifted by the upthrust available from releasing a gas stored inside them.
