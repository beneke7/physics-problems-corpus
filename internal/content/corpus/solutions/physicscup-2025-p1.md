## Solution
*Student solution by Ethan Lu, transcribed faithfully from the LaTeX source. The competition published no official solution. Notation: $s$ is the cylindrical radius and $\hat s$ the radial unit vector; $\epsilon_0,\mu_0$ are the vacuum permittivity and permeability (the problem's $\mu$ is taken as $\mu_0$).*

### Setup

Since the balls are non-magnetic there is no magnetic interaction between them. However, the Lorentz force acts on the charges in the rotating ball because they are in a magnetic field. The charges rearrange until the force from the electric field counteracts the Lorentz force, so there is an electrostatic interaction between the balls.

Since $R \ll L$, the lowest-order term of the multipole expansion is a good approximation for the electric fields of the balls. The electric field from the rotating ball induces an electric dipole in the stationary ball. Since the dipole moment is on the order of the electric field at $L$, the field from that dipole back at the rotating ball is of even lower order than the field at $L$, so the field from external sources in the rotating ball is approximately $0$.

Thus the force from the electric field of the rotating ball on the stationary ball is a good approximation of the interaction force. The induced dipole moment is oriented so that the interaction force is attractive.

### Justification for $\vec B \approx B\hat x$ in the rotating ball

Because the charge density in the rotating ball is non-zero, there is a magnetic field from the movement of the charges due to its rotation, and a magnetic field from the free currents in the ball. The following verifies the ansatz that these effects are negligible (so $\vec B = B\hat x$ in the ball) is approximately the real solution for $\vec B$.

The Lorentz force per charge will be $q\vec v\times\vec B = q(\vec\omega\times\vec r)\times\vec B = q\omega B(\hat x\times\vec r)\times\hat x = q\omega B(\vec r - (\vec r\cdot\hat x)\hat x)$, which in cylindrical coordinates $(s,\theta,\phi)$ is $q\omega B s\,\hat s$. Since the force from the electric field must counteract the Lorentz force, $q\vec E = -q\omega B s\,\hat s \Rightarrow \vec E = -\omega B s\,\hat s$. From this formula, magnetic-field contributions from currents and rotation can be calculated.

#### Magnetic field from rotation

Let $B_r$ denote the magnetic field generated by rotation (at an arbitrary point). The electric field inside the rotating ball is proportional to $B$, and therefore so is the charge density. The charge density is proportional to the current from rotation, and so proportional to the magnetic field from rotation $B_r$ (tl;dr $B_r \propto B$). The other relevant variables are $\mu_0,\epsilon_0,\omega,R$, with the only dimensionless combination (up to exponent) being $\sqrt{\mu_0\epsilon_0}\,\omega R = \omega R/c$, where $c$ is the speed of light. So $\frac{B_r}{B} = f\!\left(\frac{\omega R}{c}\right)$. Taking the fastest point on the ball to be much slower than the speed of light, the ball rotates "slowly", so its magnetic field is "small"; more precisely $\frac{B_r}{B}$ is small.

#### Magnetic field from currents

Let $B_c$ denote the magnetic field generated by current (at an arbitrary point). $B_c \propto J \propto E \propto B$, where $J$ is the current density. Since $B_c$ also depends on the quantities $\mu_0,\omega,R,\rho$: $\frac{B_c}{B} = f(\mu_0,\omega,R,\rho)$. Since these quantities have only one dimensionless combination (up to exponent), $\frac{\rho}{\omega\mu_0 R^2}$, which is $\gg 1$ as given in the problem ($R\ll\sqrt{\rho/\mu\omega}$). Since the resistivity is "large" the current is "small", so the magnetic field from the current is "small"; more precisely $\frac{B_c}{B}$ is small.

### Interaction force calculation

The total dipole moment has the same symmetries as the electric field. Since the electric field is invariant under rotations about the $x$ axis and reflections about the $yz$ plane at $x=0$, the total dipole moment must be $0$; the next term in the multipole expansion is the quadrupole.

#### Finding the field from the rotating ball

Since a quadrupole potential is suspected, possible quadrupole potentials are investigated. Using a process analogous to how dipole potentials are constructed, place a dipole of dipole moment $p\hat x$ at the origin and $-p\hat x$ at $(d,0,0)$, taking $\lim_{d\to 0}$ of the potential. Since the potential associated with a dipole moment $\vec p$ is $\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{\vec p\cdot\vec r}{r^3}$, the potential of the quadrupole is
$$ \lim_{d\to 0}\frac{p}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\!\left(\frac{x}{(x^2+y^2+z^2)^{\frac32}} - \frac{x-d}{((x-d)^2+y^2+z^2)^{\frac32}}\right) = \lim_{d\to 0}\frac{pd}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{d}{dx}\!\left(\frac{x}{(x^2+y^2+z^2)^{\frac32}}\right). $$
Fix $pd$ to a constant $A$; then the limit becomes
$$ \frac{A}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{d}{dx}\!\left(\frac{x}{(x^2+y^2+z^2)^{\frac32}}\right) = \frac{A}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{-2x^2+y^2+z^2}{(x^2+y^2+z^2)^{\frac52}} = \frac{A}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{-3x^2+r^2}{r^5}. $$

Notice (miraculously) that integrating the electric field inside the ball gives the potential to be $-\tfrac12\omega B s^2 = -\tfrac12\omega B(r^2-x^2) + C_1$, so on the surface of the ball, $-\tfrac12\omega B(R^2 - x^2) + C_1 = \tfrac12\omega B x^2 + C_2$. So if $A = -\frac{5}{2}\pi\epsilon_0\omega B R^5$ then the quadrupole potential equals the potential of the ball on the surface up to an additive constant. A point charge at the center has an equipotential surface on the surface of the ball, therefore the potential of the rotating ball on the surface can be expressed as the sum of the potentials of a point charge and the quadrupole constructed above. Since this satisfies the Dirichlet boundary condition, by the uniqueness theorem the potential outside the rotating ball is the sum of the potential of a point charge and the quadrupole. Since the rotating ball has no net charge, the potential outside is simply the potential of the quadrupole constructed above:
$$ \boxed{\; V_r = \frac{1}{6}\omega B R^5\,\frac{2x^2 - y^2 - z^2}{(x^2+y^2+z^2)^{\frac52}}. \;} $$

#### Induced dipole moment on the stationary ball

The electric field at the stationary ball is in the $x$ direction by rotational symmetry, and it is an approximately uniform electric field $\vec E = E\hat x$. The potential from the ball on itself must then be $E(x-L)$ up to an additive constant (for the same reasons as in the previous section, this constant can be ignored). The potential of a dipole with dipole moment $p$ at $L$ is $\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{p(x-L)}{r^3}$, so if $p = 4\pi\epsilon_0 R^3 E$ then the potential from the dipole moment equals the potential from the ball on its surface. This constitutes a Dirichlet boundary condition and again, by uniqueness, the potential outside the ball equals the dipole potential, so its dipole moment is
$$ -4\pi\epsilon_0 R^3\left.\frac{dV_r}{dx}\right|_{(L,0,0)} = 4\pi\epsilon_0\omega B R^8\,\frac{1}{L^4} \quad\text{so}\quad \boxed{\; p = 4\pi\epsilon_0\omega B R^8\,\frac{1}{L^4}. \;} $$

#### Force calculation

The magnitude of the force from an electric field on 2 point charges with charge $q$ and $-q$ separated by a small distance in the $x$ direction is
$$ |q\vec E(x) - q\vec E(x+d)| \approx \left|\vec E(x) - q\!\left(\vec E + \frac{d\vec E}{dx}d\right)\right| = \left|qd\frac{d\vec E}{dx}\right| = \left|p\frac{d\vec E}{dx}\right| = \left|p\frac{d^2 V_r}{dx^2}\right|, $$
because by symmetry $E_y = E_z = 0$ along the $x$ axis. The interaction force is then a force of attraction with magnitude
$$ \boxed{\; |F| = \left|\frac{16\pi\epsilon_0\,\omega^2 B^2 R^{13}}{L^9}\right|. \;} $$
