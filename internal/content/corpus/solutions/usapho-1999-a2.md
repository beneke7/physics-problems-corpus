A2. Assume a charge of $Q_{2}$ at $\mathrm{x}=d$ along the $x$-axis. The total potential a distance $c$ from the origin is

$$
V=k \frac{Q_{2}}{R_{2}}+k \frac{q}{R_{1}}
$$

where k is Coulomb's constant and $R_{I}$ and $R_{2}$ can be found using the law of cosines.

$$
R_{1}=\sqrt{b^{2}-2 b c \cos \theta+c^{2}}
$$

and
![](https://cdn.mathpix.com/cropped/112d23ef-1068-4ef8-abd5-030c70654242-2.jpg?height=524&width=878&top_left_y=1669&top_left_x=1090)

$$
R_{2}=\sqrt{d^{2}-2 d c \cos \theta+c^{2}}
$$

Since $\mathrm{V}=0$,

$$
0=k \frac{Q_{2}}{R_{2}}+k \frac{q}{R_{1}}
$$

Therefore

$$
\frac{Q_{2}}{R_{2}}=-\frac{q}{R_{1}}
$$

This must be true for all points a distance c from the origin. In particular at $\theta=0$ or $\mathrm{x}=+\mathrm{c}$ we
have

$$
\frac{Q_{2}}{(c-d)}=-\frac{q}{(b-c)}
$$

At $\theta=180^{\circ}=\pi$ or $x=-c$ we have

$$
\frac{Q_{2}}{(c+d)}=-\frac{q}{(b+c)}
$$

Solving equations (2) and (3) simultaneously for $Q_{2}$ and $d$, we have

$$
Q_{2}=-\frac{c}{b} q \quad \text { at } \quad d=\frac{c^{2}}{b}
$$

Substituting $d$ into $R_{2}$, we have

$$
R_{2}=\sqrt{\left(c^{2} / b\right)^{2}-2\left(c^{2} / b\right) c \cos \theta+c^{2}}=(c / b) \sqrt{c^{2}-2 b c \cos \theta+b^{2}}=(c / b) R_{1}
$$

for any angle $\theta_{\text {, i.e. any point a distance } c \text { from the origin. Substituting } Q_{2} \text { and } R_{2} \text { into (1), }}$

$$
V=k \frac{(-c / b) q}{(c / b) R_{1}}+k \frac{q}{R_{1}}=0
$$
