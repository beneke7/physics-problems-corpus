## Hint

Describe the phenomenon in the frame of reference of the liquid. Consider first the transformation of the electric and magnetic fields when they are viewed in two different frames of reference, one moving at speed $v_{0}$ relative to the other. Take $v_{0}$ as being much less than the velocity of light and ignore relativistic effects.

## Solution

The Lorentz force

$$
\mathbf{F}=Q(\mathbf{E}+\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{B})
$$

acts on a small body of charge $Q$ moving with velocity $\mathbf{v}$ in a field of electric field strength $\mathbf{E}$ and magnetic field strength $\mathbf{B}$. In a frame of reference moving at velocity $\mathbf{v}_{0}$ relative to the original one, the velocity of the particle is $\mathbf{v}^{\prime}=\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{v}_{0}$. If all physical quantities in this new reference frame are denoted by primes, the expression for its Lorentz force is

$$
\mathbf{F}^{\prime}=Q^{\prime}\left(\mathbf{E}^{\prime}+\mathbf{v}^{\prime} \times \mathbf{B}^{\prime}\right) .
$$

Now compare the two frames of reference. Any force acting on the particle which can be detected (e.g. through the acceleration it produces) cannot change, i.e. $\mathbf{F}^{\prime}=\mathbf{F}$. The same is true for the electric charge: $Q=Q^{\prime}$. (If particle charges depended on their velocity then an initially neutral body would show a net electric charge when heated-no trace of this is seen in nature.)
Transforming the velocities yields

$$
Q(\mathbf{E}+\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{B})=Q\left(\mathbf{E}^{\prime}-\mathbf{v}_{0} \times \mathbf{B}^{\prime}+\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{B}^{\prime}\right) .
$$

This relation has to be satisfied for any $\mathbf{v}$, and specifically for stationary particles. Therefore

$$
\mathbf{B}^{\prime}=\mathbf{B} \quad \text { leading to } \quad \mathbf{E}^{\prime}=\mathbf{E}+\mathbf{v}_{0} \times \mathbf{B} .
$$

It can be seen that electric and magnetic fields are not independent physical quantities, their values depend on the (velocity of the) frame of reference in which phenomena are described (see also P184). We now apply these general relationships (twice) to the problem in hand.

Consider the frame of reference of the liquid. To get to this frame from the capacitor's frame requires a transformation with $\mathbf{v}_{0}=-\mathbf{v}$. The magnetic field in this new frame is unaltered as $\mathbf{B}^{\prime}=\mathbf{B}$, but an electric field $\mathbf{E}^{\prime}$ of strength $-\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{B}$ is also present. The stationary liquid is polarised by this field, and the electric field strength is consequently reduced by a factor $1 / \varepsilon_{\mathrm{r}}$ to $\mathbf{E}^{\prime}=-\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{B}\left(1 / \varepsilon_{\mathrm{r}}\right)$. Returning to the original (capacitor) frame of reference requires a further transformation, but this time with $\mathbf{v}_{0}$ set equal to $+\mathbf{v}$. Again the magnetic field is unchanged, $\mathbf{B}^{\prime \prime}=\mathbf{B}^{\prime}=\mathbf{B}$, but the electric field is given by

$$
\mathbf{E}^{\prime \prime}=\mathbf{E}^{\prime}+\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{B}^{\prime}=-\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{B}\left(1 / \varepsilon_{\mathrm{r}}\right)+\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{B}=\left(1-\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{\mathrm{r}}}\right) \mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{B} .
$$

Consequently, a p.d. of

$$
V=v B d\left(1-\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{\mathrm{r}}}\right)
$$

appears between the plates.
For non-polarisable materials ( $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{r}}=1$ ), this voltage is obviously zero, whilst in the case of 'easily' polarisable materials (metals), $V=v B d$. This latter equation, describing the Hall effect, can be interpreted in the following way. The charges in the conductor moving in the magnetic field are displaced by the Lorentz force. The sideways migration of charges continues until an electrostatic field strong enough to balance the Lorentz force (which is proportional to the magnetic field) has been built up. From the condition for this, $Q E=Q v B$, the above relationship, $V=E d=v B d$, can be recovered.
