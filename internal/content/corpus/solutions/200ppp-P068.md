## Hint

The centre of mass (CM) of the compasses is directly below the attachment point. If the angle between the arms were changed, the horizontal position of the CM would have to remain the same, although the positions of the CM of the individual arms would change. Use this argument to find the solution to the problem with a minimum of actual calculation.

## Solution

Using the argument described in the Hint it is possible to prove that the compasses have to be opened to the extent necessary to make the lower arm hang horizontally when the compasses are suspended, as shown in Fig. S68.1.

![](../../sources/200ppp/figures/200ppp-153.jpg)
Fig. S68.1

Starting from that situation, let us imagine for the moment that the upper arm of the compasses is fixed. If the lower arm is then bent either upwards or downwards, the horizontal position of the CM of the compasses moves towards the pivot. After the release of the upper arm the pivot moves downwards, because that is the only way that the CM of the compasses can again position itself below the attachment point. So, in either case, the vertical position of the pivot is lowered, and we can conclude that the originally described situation is the one required.

Instead of a real pair of compasses let us consider a simplified model of two identical thin rods joined by a pivot of negligible mass as shown in Fig. S68.2.

![](../../sources/200ppp/figures/200ppp-154.jpg)
Fig. S68.2

Let the angle between the arms of the compasses be $2 \theta$, and the length of each of the arms be 2 units. It is easy to find congruent angles in Fig. S68.2 and to apply the sine rule to the shaded triangle. Figure S 68.3 shows a magnified version of this triangle.

![](../../sources/200ppp/figures/200ppp-155.jpg)
Fig. S68.3

According to the sine rule it follows that

$$
\frac{\sin \theta}{1}=\frac{\sin \left(90^{\circ}-2 \theta\right)}{\sin \theta} .
$$

After a simple calculation we obtain

$$
\sin \theta=\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}},
$$

so $\theta \approx 35.3^{\circ}$, that is to say the compasses should be opened to $2 \theta \approx 70.5^{\circ}$.
Note. The result $2 \theta=2 \sin ^{-1}(1 / \sqrt{3})=\cos ^{-1}(1 / 3)$ is so simple, one might suppose that a more elegant solution exists. Actually, it is possible to find the angle $2 \theta$ with the help of the theorem of parallel transversals.

![](../../sources/200ppp/figures/200ppp-156.jpg)
Fig. S68.4

In Fig. S68.4 two verticals are drawn, one through the overall centre of mass $C$ of the compasses, and the other through the centre of mass $E$ of the upper arm alone. By considering the lines forming the angle $2 \theta$, it can be seen that the equality of $O E$ and $E B$ implies that $F G=G O$. Similar consideration of the angle $\phi$, and the fact that $D C=C E$, shows that $F G=D F$. Thus points $F$ and $G$ trisect the section $O D$, which in turn is equal to $O E$, thus implying that $O E=3 G O$. As $E G O$ is a right-angled triangle, it follows that $2 \theta=\cos ^{-1}(1 / 3) \approx 70.5^{\circ}$.
