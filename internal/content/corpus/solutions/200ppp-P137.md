## Hint

The hydrostatic pressure of the water in the flue increases the pressure of the water in the cavity, and so it boils at a temperature higher than the usual $100^{\circ} \mathrm{C}$. The relationship between the pressure and temperature of the saturated water vapour can be obtained from tables or by using the approximate law

$$
p=A \mathrm{e}^{-L_{\mathrm{m}} /(R T)} .
$$

Here $p$ is the pressure of saturated water vapour at its boiling point $T, L_{\mathrm{m}}$ is the molar heat of vaporisation of water, $R$ is the gas constant and $A$ is a constant with the dimensions of pressure. When the geyser erupts, the superheated water in the cavity reaches equilibrium again by boiling until it cools down to $100^{\circ} \mathrm{C}$.

## Solution

Assume that the cavity is located at a depth $h$ below the surface. When the flue is filled with water, the pressure in the cavity is $p=p_{0}+\rho g h$, where $p_{0}$ is the atmospheric pressure and $\rho$ is the density of water. Boiling starts at that depth when the temperature is such that the pressure of saturated water vapour $\left(A \mathrm{e}^{-L_{m} /(R T)}\right)$ is equal to $p$. The value of the molar heat of vaporisation $L_{m}$ occurring in the formula is approximately $40 \mathrm{~kJ} \mathrm{~mol}^{-1}$ and that of the molar gas constant $R$ is $8.3 \mathrm{~J} \mathrm{~mol}^{-1} \mathrm{~K}^{-1}$. Using these data, the constant $A$ can be determined from the boiling point of water ( 373 K ) at atmospheric pressure; the result is $A=4.1 \times 10^{10} \mathrm{~Pa}$.

For the purposes of the calculations, the temperature of the ground at the surface can be assumed to be $T_{0}=290 \mathrm{~K}$ and, as it increases by one degree per metre, the temperature at depth $h$ is given by $T=T_{0}+h$. The following equation can now be formulated, using the equality of the pressures when
the water starts to boil:

$$
p_{0}+\rho g h=A \mathrm{e}^{-\frac{L_{\mathrm{m}}}{R\left(T_{0}+h\right)}} .
$$

This transcendental equation can be solved by numerical methods, i.e. by carefully considered guesses! The solution is $h=197(\approx 200) \mathrm{m}$. The pressure at such a depth is approximately 20 atm and the temperature $487 \mathrm{~K}=214^{\circ} \mathrm{C}$.

When boiling starts, the first steam bubbles rise and push the water out of the flue, the pressure decreases to 1 atm and the superheated water at $214^{\circ} \mathrm{C}$ starts to boil violently, producing as much steam as is necessary to reduce its temperature to $100^{\circ} \mathrm{C}$. The temperature drop of $\Delta T=114^{\circ} \mathrm{C}$ implies an excess internal energy of $c m \Delta T$, where $c$ is the specific heat of water and $m$ is the mass of water in the cavity. This surplus energy is sufficient to produce $m_{s}=44$ tons of steam, implying

$$
c m \Delta T=L m_{s}
$$

where $L$ is the heat of vaporisation of water. After substituting the relevant data, we find the mass of the water in the cavity to be $m=207$ tons. Since the density of water is only around $850 \mathrm{~kg} \mathrm{~m}^{-3}$ at such a high temperature, this gives an approximate value of $244 \mathrm{~m}^{3}$ for the volume of the cavity.
