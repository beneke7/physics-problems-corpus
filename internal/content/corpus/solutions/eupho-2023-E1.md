## Solution

### Motivation

In the vibration isolation system of the VIRGO gravitational wave detector, repulsive magnets are used to shift the vertical oscillation frequency of the suspension from around 1.5 Hz to below 0.5 Hz. This improves the sensitivity of the detector to gravitational waves at a frequency of few Hz. (Mechanical suspension of the VIRGO gravitational wave interferometer; from NIM A 394 (1997) 397-408.)

### Derivation of modified pendulum frequency (given to students)

The motion of a physical pendulum is constrained to the $y = 0$ plane (one degree of freedom). A magnetic dipole (oriented along the $y$-axis) is attached to the pendulum such that it is located at the origin when the pendulum is in equilibrium. Two more $y$-oriented magnetic dipoles are placed on the $y$-axis at $y = \pm d$. The combined dipole moment of the magnets on the pendulum is $j_1$, the dipole moments of the external magnets are both $j_2$.

The magnetic field at position $\vec{r}$ generated by a point dipole $\vec{m}$ at the origin is:

$$\vec{B}(\vec{m}, \vec{r}) = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\cdot\left(\frac{3\vec{r}\,(\vec{m}\cdot\vec{r})}{r^5} - \frac{\vec{m}}{r^3}\right) \tag{1}$$

The $y$-component of the magnetic field generated on the $x$-axis by a dipole $(0, j_2, 0)$ located at $(0, d, 0)$ is therefore:

$$B_y(d, x) = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\cdot j_2 \cdot \frac{2d^2 - x^2}{(x^2 + d^2)^{5/2}} \tag{2}$$

The function is symmetric in $d$, so the two external magnets provide equal contributions on the $x$-axis. The first terms of a Taylor series around $x = 0$ are

$$B_y(d, x) = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\cdot j_2 \cdot\left(\frac{2}{d^3} - \frac{6x^2}{d^5} + \frac{45}{4}\frac{x^4}{d^7} + O(x^6)\right) \tag{3}$$

The potential energy of the pendulum as a function of its angle $\varphi$ is:

$$U(\varphi) = Mgs(1 - \cos\varphi) + j_1 \cdot 2 B_y(d, 2\ell\cdot\sin(\varphi/2)), \tag{4}$$

where $s$ is the distance from the magnetic pendulum COM to the pivot and $\ell$ is the distance from the pendulum magnet to the pivot. Neglecting constants and higher order terms:

$$U(\varphi) \approx Mgs\,\varphi^2/2 + \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\cdot j_1 \cdot 2j_2 \cdot\left(-\frac{6}{d^5}\ell^2\varphi^2\right), \tag{5}$$

$$U(\varphi) \approx \left(Mgs - \frac{6\mu_0}{\pi}\cdot j_1 \cdot j_2 \cdot\frac{\ell^2}{d^5}\right)\cdot\varphi^2/2. \tag{6}$$

The kinetic energy of the pendulum is $T(\dot\varphi) = I\dot\varphi^2/2$. From $\partial_\varphi U(\varphi) = \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\partial_{\dot\varphi}T(\dot\varphi)$ we find the natural frequency

$$\omega^2 = \left(Mgs - \frac{6\mu_0}{\pi}\cdot j_1 \cdot j_2 \cdot\frac{\ell^2}{d^5}\right)\Big/ I \tag{7}$$

We can write

$$\omega^2 = \omega_0^2 \pm \omega_\mathrm{mag}^2 \tag{8}$$

with $\omega_0^2 = Mgs/I$ and $\omega_\mathrm{mag}^2 = \frac{6\mu_0}{I\pi}\cdot j_1 \cdot j_2 \cdot\frac{\ell^2}{d^5}$, where the plus sign is for attractive external magnet polarity and the minus sign for repulsive external magnet polarity.

### Task E1.1 - Mass of magnets and pendulum body (1.0 pts)

Without scales for weight measurements, there are multiple ways to determine the mass ratio between magnets and pendulum:

A) finding the center of mass of the pendulum with and without magnets (or with magnets in different positions);

B) finding the tilt angle of the pendulum with magnets offset horizontally (very precise by laser angle measurement);

C) measuring the pendulum frequency as a function of vertical magnet position, and using e.g. the position of fastest oscillation.

The methods allow very different precision (with B best and C worst typically). Full score is achievable with any method if the numerical result is good.

All pendulums were weighed on an analytical balance: $M_\mathrm{pen} = (44.7 \pm 1.7)\,\mathrm{g}$ (mean and standard deviation). The mass of the two pendulum magnets agrees well with the specifications $M_\mathrm{mag} = (7.68 \pm 0.01)\,\mathrm{g}$. The nominal mass ratio follows as

$$M/m = 5.82 \pm 0.22. \tag{9}$$

Since the magnet mass is more consistent between setups than the pendulum mass (and both measurements are fully anticorrelated by the given sum $M_\mathrm{pen} + M_\mathrm{mag} = 52.3\,\mathrm{g}$), points are awarded only for the determined magnet mass.

**A: center of mass method.** Balancing torques around the new center of mass $S'$:

$$M\cdot SS' = m\cdot S'P \tag{10}$$

With $SS' = (1.6 \pm 0.1)\,\mathrm{cm}$ and $S'P = (9.4 \pm 0.1)\,\mathrm{cm}$ one finds $M/m \approx 5.9 \pm 0.4$.

**B: tilt-angle method.** Balancing torques with the pendulum tilted by laser angle measurement,

$$M\cdot s_0\cdot\sin\alpha = m\cdot\sqrt{x_m^2 + y_m^2}\cdot\sin\beta \tag{15}$$

where

$$\tan(\alpha + \beta) = x_m/y_m. \tag{16}$$

With $s_0 = (11.4 \pm 0.1)\,\mathrm{cm}$, $x_m = (7.7 \pm 0.1)\,\mathrm{cm}$, $y_m = (18.0 \pm 0.1)\,\mathrm{cm}$, $d = (55 \pm 1)\,\mathrm{cm}$, and $\delta y = (5.9 \pm 0.1)\,\mathrm{cm}$, we obtain $\alpha = 6.12^\circ$, $\beta = 17.0^\circ$, and $M/m = 4.7 \pm 0.4$.

**C: frequency vs vertical magnet position.** From the relations $I\cdot\omega_0^2 = Mgs_0$ (20) and $(I + m\ell^2)\cdot\omega_1^2 = (M + m)gs_1$ (21), the mass ratio can be expressed as

$$M/m = \frac{\ell^2/g - s_1^2/\omega_1^2}{s_1^2/\omega_1^2 - s_0^2/\omega_0^2} = 6.1 \pm 0.3. \tag{22}$$

After a scan of different $y$-values: $y_0 = (10 \pm 3)\,\mathrm{cm}$, $\omega_\mathrm{max} = (7.65 \pm 0.05)\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$, $\omega_1 = (7.50 \pm 0.06)\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$, $\Omega = (9.28 \pm 0.04)\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$, giving $M/m = 6.7\ldots14$. This method is not precise enough with the relatively high mass ratio of the setup.

*Table of physical parameters (from the source):* $M_\mathrm{pen} = (44.7 \pm 1.7)\,\mathrm{g}$, $M_\mathrm{mag} = (7.68 \pm 0.03)\,\mathrm{g}$, $\ell = (23.0 \pm 0.1)\,\mathrm{cm}$, $s_0 = (12.0 \pm 0.1)\,\mathrm{cm}$, $s_1 = (13.6 \pm 0.1)\,\mathrm{cm}$, $T_0 = 2\pi/\omega_0 = (863 \pm 5)\,\mathrm{ms}$, $T_1 = 2\pi/\omega_1 = (885 \pm 3)\,\mathrm{ms}$, $I = (1.38 \pm 0.02)\,\mathrm{g\cdot m^2}$, $j_1 = (0.96 \pm 0.01)\,\mathrm{A\cdot m^2}$, $j_2 = (2.30 \pm 0.03)\,\mathrm{A\cdot m^2}$.

### Task E1.2 - Magnetic dipole moments (4.0 pts)

**a) Data collection.** The accessible frequency range is maximized by using both possible external dipole orientations. The closest distance in attractive orientation is limited by stability of the magnets in the rail against sliding towards the pendulum, around $d = 7\,\mathrm{cm}$. In repulsive mode it is given by the full compensation point near $d = 8.4\,\mathrm{cm}$ (distances down to around $9\,\mathrm{cm}$ are practical). For closer spacings in repulsive mode, the pendulum moves in a double-well potential with ill-defined behavior around zero amplitude; Eqn. (8) gives a negative $\omega^2$ and is not valid for oscillations around the off-set minimum, so these data points are not useful.

Since one needs to plot $\omega^2$ vs $d^{-5}$, small $d$ need to be sampled much more finely to spread the data points in $d^{-5}$. Ideally one pre-calculates an equal spacing, e.g. $(0^{-0.2}, 0.25^{-0.2}, 0.5^{-0.2}, 0.75^{-0.2}, 1^{-0.2})\cdot d_0 = (\infty, 1.32, 1.15, 1.06, 1)\cdot d_0$.

**b) Specific magnetization.** Both attractive and repulsive datasets can be combined in one plot by counting repulsive magnet spacings as negative, absorbing the sign at $\pm\omega_\mathrm{mag}^2$. Plotting $\omega^2$ vs $d^{-5}$, one fits a linear relation with slope $k = \frac{6\mu_0\ell^2}{I\pi}\cdot j_1 \cdot j_2$ and intercept $\omega_1^2$: $k = (2.14 \pm 0.05)\cdot10^6\,\mathrm{cm^5/s^2}$. To extract the dipole moment,

$$I = \frac{(M_\mathrm{pend} + M_\mathrm{mag})gs_1}{\,} = (1.38 \pm 0.02)\,\mathrm{g\cdot m^2}. \tag{24}$$

The dipole moment product follows as

$$j_1\cdot j_2 = k\cdot\frac{I\pi}{6\mu_0\ell^2} = (2.33 \pm 0.05)\,(\mathrm{A\,m^2})^2. \tag{25}$$

Since $j_2/j_1 = 2.4$ is given, the dipole moments separately are

$$j_1 = \sqrt{j_1\cdot j_2/2.4} = (0.98 \pm 0.01)\,\mathrm{A\,m^2}, \tag{26}$$

$$j_2 = \sqrt{j_1\cdot j_2 \cdot 2.4} = (2.36 \pm 0.03)\,\mathrm{A\,m^2}. \tag{27}$$

With the true mass of the pendulum magnets $M_\mathrm{magnets} = 7.68\,\mathrm{g}$, the specific magnetization is

$$j_1/M_\mathrm{magnets} = (0.128 \pm 0.030)\,\mathrm{A\,m^2/g}. \tag{28}$$

The expected value, from manufacturer values of remanence (1.29 T–1.32 T) and density (7.4–7.5 g/cm³) of NdFeB–N42, is

$$\frac{B_r}{\mu_0\cdot\rho} = 0.137\,\mathrm{A\,m^2/g} - 0.142\,\mathrm{A\,m^2/g}. \tag{29}$$

The ~10% reduction of the observed specific magnetization may be explained by edge effects in the small magnets, where magnetization may not be homogeneous. There is a hint of edge effects also from the magnetic moment ratio between external and pendulum dipole moments: 2.4 measured with a magnetometer, higher than the volume ratio 2.2 measured without the coatings.

### Task E1.3 - Unknown external magnets (3.0 pts)

**a) Data collection.** By experimentation one finds that the unknown magnets are symmetric under a $180^\circ$ rotation, not anti-symmetric as the dipoles. One can still obtain both attraction and repulsion, either by swapping the unknown magnets (which are oppositely magnetized to each other) or by flipping the pendulum magnets. As the overall field strength is weaker, distances down to $d = 5\,\mathrm{cm}$ are possible in attractive mode, and about $d = 5.5\,\mathrm{cm}$ in repulsive mode. Attractive and repulsive modes cannot be combined to increase precision, but serve as independent measurements; only the attractive mode is selected for its higher accessible $d$-range and faster frequencies (higher relative precision).

**b) Power law exponent.** Plotting the absolute value of the squared magnetic frequency shift $\ln|\omega^2 - \omega_1^2|$ versus the magnet distance on a double-logarithmic plot, one fits a linear relation with slope $\alpha = -6.33 \pm 0.32$, indicating

$$\omega_\mathrm{mag}^2 \propto d^{-6}.$$

**c) Dipole configuration.** Since the exponent of the magnetic frequency shift is one higher than for the dipole magnets, we conclude that the exponent of the magnetic field as a function of distance is also one higher than for the dipole law, $B \propto r^{-4}$. (In fact the squared magnetic frequency shift follows the curvature of the magnetic field, and the derivatives add two to the power law exponent.) We are looking for a magnetic quadrupole configuration. This can be created by an arrangement of equal and opposite dipole magnets (canceling the total dipole moment). The simplest solution consistent with the package consists of two coaxial and opposite cylindrical dipole magnets in each unknown external magnet, one with north poles facing out, one with south poles facing out. Another valid solution is made of cylinders with radial magnetization (south poles towards the symmetry axis, north poles outwards, and vice versa); this would be harder to build but yields indistinguishable magnets to leading order.

### Task E1.4 - Nonlinear pendulum (2.0 pts)

**a) Compensation distance.** A precise way of finding the compensation distance is from the analysis of E1.2: the $x$-intercept of the regression line, with slope $k = (2.14 \pm 0.05)\cdot10^6\,\mathrm{cm^5/s^2}$ and $y$-intercept $\omega_1^2 = (50.3 \pm 0.3)\,\mathrm{s^{-2}}$. We find $d_\mathrm{comp}^5 = k/\omega_1^2$, or $d_\mathrm{comp} = (8.43 \pm 0.05)\,\mathrm{cm}$. Another possibility is to manually approach the compensation point and check for the absence of two separate minima. A less useful method is recording the angular separation $\Delta\varphi$ of the split minima for $d < d_\mathrm{comp}$ as a function of $d$; this relation is strongly curved and not suited for precise extrapolation to $\Delta\varphi = 0$.

**b) Period power law.** Near full compensation of the quadratic terms of gravitational and magnetic potential, the pendulum period depends on amplitude as follows:

- For small amplitudes the period is constant, given by the small remaining quadratic potential term from imperfect compensation.
- In an intermediate amplitude range, the magnetic quartic potential dominates, leading to a theoretical dependence $T(\varphi) \propto \varphi^{-1}$ with amplitude $\varphi$.
- For large amplitudes, the influence of the localized magnetic potential is small and the period approaches that of the undisturbed pendulum.

The extent of the intermediate amplitude range depends on the quality of compensation, quantified e.g. by the ratio $T_0/T(\varphi = 0)$ of the compensated to uncompensated small-amplitude period. Typically the theoretical exponent of $-1$ is not reached in experiment, because the quartic potential does not fully dominate for any amplitude region. The expected maximum slope of the power law can be calculated numerically as a function of $T_0/T(\varphi = 0)$. A measured power-law slope of $a = -0.63 \pm 0.02$ was obtained in the intermediate range.
